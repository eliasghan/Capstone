# Round 1 — Querying Strategy Reflection

*Context: these answers describe round 1, where each proposed point is based solely on the initial data supplied (10–40 points per function) — no new realised outputs have returned yet. Every function is a maximisation over [0,1]^d.*

## 1. The main principle behind each query point

The single governing principle was to **maximise an acquisition function that trades off exploitation (high predicted output) against exploration (high model uncertainty)**, and to set *where* on that trade-off each function sits according to (a) what its description told us about its shape and (b) how tiny the budget is (10 sequential points). Concretely, three ideas drove every pick:

**Model the surface first, then let the acquisition choose.** For each function I fit a Gaussian Process (Matérn-5/2 kernel, one lengthscale per dimension) to all points seen so far. The GP gives a predicted mean (what we expect) and a standard deviation (how unsure we are) everywhere. Every query point is simply the location that maximises the acquisition function over that posterior — so the "decision" is a principled optimisation, not a guess.

**Choose the exploration/exploitation tilt per function by its known shape.** Where the description implied a *single smooth peak* I used Expected Improvement with a small margin — i.e. near-pure **exploitation**, walking uphill from the best region (F5, and to a lesser degree F3, F6, F7). Where the description implied *many local optima, sparsity, or noise* I used Upper Confidence Bound with a deliberately large weight on uncertainty — i.e. **exploration**, deliberately sampling where the model is unsure so we don't commit early to a false peak (F1, F2, F4, F8). F1 is the extreme case: with almost no signal in the data it is essentially pure **uncertainty-driven discovery**.

**Make "high output" and "the incumbent" trustworthy before comparing to them.** Two supporting heuristics matter here. First, outputs were standardised (and log-transformed for F5, whose values span 0.1 to ~1089) so the GP isn't distorted by scale. Second, for the noisy functions the "current best" that EI tries to beat is taken as the best *posterior-mean* value, not the single best raw reading — so one lucky noisy measurement can't set an unbeatable bar and freeze the search.

Diversity of samples is handled *implicitly* rather than as a separate objective: pushing the uncertainty weight up (high β in UCB, or a positive margin in EI) naturally spreads points into unexplored regions, which is the right form of "diversity" for a sequential one-point-at-a-time budget.

## 2. The most challenging functions to query, and what would have helped

**F1 (2-D contamination) was the hardest, by a wide margin.** Nine of its ten initial points read essentially zero and the tenth is barely negative, so the function is a flat, near-signal-free surface with the reward hidden in a tiny region we haven't hit. This breaks the normal machinery: Expected Improvement collapses to ~0 everywhere (there is no "improvement" to expect over a flat zero), so I had to fall back to pure uncertainty sampling / high-β UCB just to *discover* where any signal is. What would have helped most: a larger or space-filling initial design to raise the chance of landing near a source; any prior on how many sources exist, their rough locations, or their strength; and confirmation of whether the zeros are true zeros or noise. A smoother "distance-to-source" signal (rather than near-binary proximity) would also give the GP a gradient to follow.

**F8 (8-D), and to a lesser extent F7 (6-D), were challenging for the opposite reason — dimensionality.** Even with 40 initial points, 8 dimensions is far too large a volume to model globally, so a single global GP is under-determined and its optimism in unexplored corners is untrustworthy. I handled this by going *local* — a trust-region search around the current best — and by leaning on the fitted per-dimension lengthscales to see which inputs actually matter (several of F8's lengthscales saturate at their upper bound, suggesting some inputs barely move the output). What would have helped: knowing the true *effective* dimensionality or which hyperparameters are influential, and literature-based sensible sub-ranges for each input so the search doesn't waste points in regions no reasonable setting would use.

**F5 was challenging in a subtler way — extreme output scale.** Its yield spans four orders of magnitude and is heavily right-skewed, which makes the raw GP fit unstable and pushes the acquisition toward the *corners* of the input box, where the model extrapolates a huge but very uncertain payoff. The log-transform tames this, but the corner-seeking behaviour is a flag to watch: it is the model saying "I can't rule out something enormous out here." Knowing whether the optimum is interior, and having a couple of points that bracket the peak, would sharpen it quickly.

Across all functions, the two pieces of information that would have helped universally are the **noise level / whether outputs are deterministic** (it changes how much to trust each reading and whether to ever replicate a point), and any **known structure** — monotonicity, separability, plausible input ranges, or the number of optima — since with only 10 queries, each such fact is worth several evaluations.

## 3. How the strategy will adapt in future rounds

The whole design is built to adapt, because the process is sequential: the GP is refit after every returned output, and the exploration/exploitation tilt is meant to move over the ten weeks — more exploratory early to reduce uncertainty, then pure exploitation in the final 2–3 rounds to convert what we've learned into the best possible point. The concrete decision rule tying *observed performance and uncertainty* to the next action is:

- **Improved last round, and uncertainty near the best point is still meaningful → keep exploiting locally** (lower β / lower the EI margin, or shrink the trust region) to refine the peak.
- **No improvement for 2–3 rounds, and uncertainty elsewhere is still high → the current basin looks exhausted; explore** (raise β / the EI margin, widen or move the trust region) to test a different region.
- **No improvement and uncertainty is low everywhere → that function has effectively converged;** its remaining queries are better spent on exploration or, if truly flat, deprioritised relative to functions still improving.

Per function, the specific triggers are: **F1** flips from UCB-exploration to EI-exploitation the moment any non-zero reading appears (while keeping a shot or two in reserve, since there may be multiple sources); if it stays all-zero, widen the spread further. **F2** — estimate the noise from nearby/repeated points, and if it's large, trust the posterior mean more and consider replicating the best point rather than chasing new ones. **F5** — if the corner proposals come back low, that falsifies the model's corner-optimism, so contract the search toward the region around the current best and drop the exploration margin. **F4 and F8** — use the trust region as the dial: tighten it while improving, grow or relocate it when stalled, and drop dimensions whose ARD lengthscales say they don't matter. **F3, F6, F7** — standard EI schedule: raise the margin if progress stalls, drive it to zero for the final exploitation rounds.

The unifying signal is the acquisition value itself: when the best achievable Expected Improvement for a function becomes negligible, the model is telling us it no longer expects to beat the incumbent — that is the cue to either switch that function to exploration or accept convergence and concentrate effort where the expected gains are still large.
